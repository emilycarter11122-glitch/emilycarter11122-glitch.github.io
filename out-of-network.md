# Out-of-Network Medical Billing and the No Surprises Act: What Providers Should Know?

Out-of-network medical billing can create complicated challenges for healthcare providers, particularly when insurance plans apply different reimbursement rules to services delivered outside their networks. Since the No Surprises Act took effect in 2022, providers also need to understand additional requirements concerning patient protections, balance billing, disclosures, and payment disputes. Effective **Healthcare Denial Management** is therefore only one part of a broader billing process that requires accurate claims, proper documentation, payer communication, and awareness of applicable federal and state requirements.

For physicians, practice managers, and other healthcare professionals, understanding how the No Surprises Act interacts with out-of-network billing can help reduce avoidable billing problems and support more consistent revenue-cycle processes. The rules do not eliminate every out-of-network payment dispute, but they establish specific protections and procedures for situations covered by the law.

## What Is Out-of-Network Medical Billing?

An out-of-network provider does not have a contractual agreement with a patient's health plan to provide services at negotiated in-network rates. Depending on the patient's insurance policy and the type of service, the provider may receive a different reimbursement amount than an in-network provider.

Out-of-network billing can occur in planned care, emergency situations, hospital-based services, and other healthcare encounters. However, providers cannot assume that being out of network automatically allows them to bill the patient for the difference between their charge and the insurer's payment.

The No Surprises Act introduced federal protections that restrict surprise billing in certain circumstances. For example, emergency services generally receive protection even when the provider is out of network. Certain non-emergency services provided at an in-network facility can also fall under the law's protections.

## How the No Surprises Act Affects Providers

The No Surprises Act was designed primarily to protect patients from unexpected out-of-network bills. For providers, this means billing procedures need to account for situations in which balance billing is prohibited.

### Emergency Services

One of the most important areas is emergency care. Under the federal protections, patients generally cannot be charged more than applicable in-network cost-sharing for covered emergency services simply because the provider is out of network.

Providers should therefore identify claims involving emergency services and determine whether the No Surprises Act applies before attempting to transfer unpaid balances to patients. The patient's insurance coverage and the specific circumstances of the encounter can affect how the requirements apply.

### Certain Services at In-Network Facilities

The law also addresses certain out-of-network services delivered at in-network facilities. Ancillary services, such as some anesthesia, radiology, pathology, and similar services, can be subject to protections even when the individual provider is out of network.

This is particularly important for hospital-based physicians and specialists who may treat patients at facilities where they do not have direct control over the patient's facility network status. Providers should review the applicable requirements before billing a patient for an out-of-network balance.

## Patient Consent and Notice Requirements

In certain circumstances, an out-of-network provider may be permitted to obtain patient consent for out-of-network treatment and associated billing. However, the notice-and-consent process has specific requirements, and it does not apply to every type of service.

The federal rules require applicable disclosures to be understandable and provided within required timeframes. Providers should not treat a generic financial agreement as automatically satisfying the No Surprises Act's requirements.

Documentation is especially important. A practice should retain evidence showing what information was provided to the patient, when it was provided, and whether the required consent was obtained when applicable.

Because exceptions exist for certain services, providers should verify the current federal requirements and any applicable state requirements rather than relying on a single general billing policy.

## Good Faith Estimates for Uninsured and Self-Pay Patients

The No Surprises Act also established requirements involving uninsured and self-pay patients.

When an uninsured or self-pay consumer schedules an item or service, the provider or facility generally must provide a good faith estimate of expected charges. An estimate must also be provided when the consumer requests one, even if the service has not yet been scheduled.

This makes accurate pre-service information an important part of the billing workflow. Practices should maintain processes for generating estimates, communicating them to patients, and retaining the related documentation.

The federal patient-provider dispute process can become relevant when an uninsured or self-pay patient receives a bill that is at least $400 higher than the expected charges shown on the good faith estimate. Providers may be asked to supply the estimate, the bill, and supporting documentation during that process.

## Managing Out-of-Network Payment Disputes

Not every disagreement between an out-of-network provider and an insurer can be resolved through ordinary claim appeals. For certain services covered by the No Surprises Act, the Federal Independent Dispute Resolution process may be available.

Generally, when a qualifying payment dispute remains unresolved, the parties first enter an open negotiation period. CMS currently describes this period as 30 business days. If the parties do not reach an agreement, an eligible dispute can proceed to the Federal IDR process under the applicable rules and deadlines.

During IDR, the provider or facility and the health plan submit payment offers and supporting information to a certified IDR entity. The certified entity makes a determination under the applicable federal process, and the parties are required to follow the resulting determination.

Providers should therefore avoid treating IDR as a routine substitute for ordinary claim follow-up. Before initiating a dispute, billing staff should determine whether the claim is eligible, confirm the required timelines, gather supporting documentation, and follow the current submission procedures.

## The Role of Documentation in Out-of-Network Billing

Documentation can have a major impact on how effectively a practice manages out-of-network claims.

Important records may include:

* Patient insurance information
* Eligibility and benefit verification
* Provider and facility network information
* Claim forms and claim submission records
* Remittance advice and payment information
* Denial or payment notices
* Patient notices and applicable consent documentation
* Good faith estimates for uninsured or self-pay patients
* Correspondence with the payer
* Records supporting a payment dispute

A consistent documentation process can make it easier to determine why a claim was underpaid or denied and whether additional action is appropriate.

## Connecting No Surprises Compliance With Denial Management

Out-of-network claims can encounter denials or reduced payments for many reasons, including eligibility problems, coding discrepancies, missing information, payer processing issues, and contractual or regulatory considerations.

This is where a structured denial workflow becomes useful. **Healthcare Denial Management** can involve reviewing denial codes, identifying recurring causes, correcting eligible claims, submitting appeals when appropriate, and monitoring trends across payers and service categories.

The objective should not simply be to resubmit every denied claim. Each denial should be reviewed to determine whether the claim contains a correctable error, requires additional documentation, is subject to an appeal process, or may involve a payment dispute governed by the No Surprises Act.

## Practical Steps for Providers

A practical approach to out-of-network billing can include several stages.

### 1. Verify Coverage Before Services

Where possible, verify the patient's insurance, network status, benefits, and applicable cost-sharing requirements before scheduled care. Verification does not replace compliance with the No Surprises Act, but it can help identify potential billing issues earlier.

### 2. Identify Protected Services

Billing teams should recognize encounters that may fall under federal surprise-billing protections, particularly emergency services and certain services provided at in-network facilities.

### 3. Maintain Accurate Records

Keep copies of relevant notices, estimates, authorizations, consent documents, claims, remittance information, and payer communications. Complete records can be important when a payment disagreement develops.

### 4. Review Denials Individually

A denial should be investigated rather than automatically resubmitted. Reviewing the reason code, claim details, payer response, and supporting documentation can help determine the appropriate next step.

### 5. Track Payment Disputes and Deadlines

When a qualifying No Surprises Act payment dispute is identified, providers should pay close attention to applicable negotiation and IDR deadlines. CMS notes that the Federal IDR process has specific timing requirements following the open negotiation period.

## Recent Changes to the Federal IDR Process

Providers should also be aware that the Federal IDR process continues to evolve. In May 2026, federal agencies finalized updated IDR operational rules intended to streamline communication and clarify processes for providers, facilities, payers, and certified IDR entities.

CMS also announced that the Federal IDR Gateway is scheduled to replace single-use web forms later in 2026. The Gateway is designed to provide a centralized system for starting and responding to disputes, monitoring dispute activity, and accessing related information.

Because requirements and administrative procedures can change, practices should use current CMS guidance when handling an IDR matter rather than relying solely on older internal procedures.

## Conclusion

Out-of-network medical billing requires more than determining what an insurer has paid. Providers also need to understand when federal surprise-billing protections apply, what patient notices or consent requirements may be relevant, how good faith estimates work for uninsured and self-pay patients, and when an eligible payment dispute may proceed to Federal IDR.

A well-organized billing process combines accurate eligibility information, appropriate documentation, careful claim review, structured denial follow-up, and awareness of current regulatory procedures. Since No Surprises Act requirements and administrative processes can change, healthcare organizations should consult current CMS guidance and applicable state requirements when making compliance decisions.
